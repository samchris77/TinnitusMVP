# Sound Therapy (Tinnitus)

## TL;DR

Sound therapy is the acoustic component of [[wiki/clinical/trt]] — and can also be used as a standalone or adjunct intervention. It uses continuous background or wearable sound to reduce the contrast between tinnitus and the acoustic environment, supporting the habituation process. The type and intensity of sound, and the delivery device, depend on the patient's hearing status and presence of hyperacusis.

## What it is

Sound therapy in the TRT framework means providing structured acoustic stimulation over extended periods — at minimum 8 hours per day — to:
- Reduce the signal-to-noise ratio between tinnitus and background (making tinnitus less "loud" relative to the environment).
- Drive passive or active attention away from tinnitus.
- Reinforce the thalamic reticular nucleus (TRN) gate closure, preventing tinnitus signals from reaching conscious awareness.

Sound therapy is **not masking**. The sound level is kept *below the mixing point* — below the level at which the tinnitus is no longer audible — so that habituation can proceed. Sound that completely masks tinnitus may provide short-term relief but prevents the brain from processing and habituating to the tinnitus signal.

## Theoretical basis

See [[wiki/clinical/trt]] for the broader neurophysiological framework. Within that framework, sound therapy works by:
1. **Contrast reduction** — background noise reduces the relative salience of the tinnitus signal (S/N ratio).
2. **Passive attention diversion** — background sound draws auditory attention away from tinnitus.
3. **Soothing effects** — appropriate sounds engage the parasympathetic nervous system, reducing limbic/autonomic reactivity that drives the tinnitus vicious cycle.
4. **Habituation induction** — consistent, neutral background sound trains the vmPFC to classify tinnitus as low-priority noise.

## The three functional categories of therapeutic sound

| Category | Function | Example |
|----------|----------|---------|
| **Soothing sound** | Engage PNS, reduce anxiety/neuronal hyperactivity, restore homeostasis | Nature sounds, soft music |
| **Background sound** | Reduce tinnitus contrast (S/N ratio), passive attention diversion, drive TRT habituation | Broadband noise, white/pink/brown noise, fan |
| **Interesting sound** | Active attention diversion | Podcasts, audiobooks, music the patient engages with |

## Requirements for sound therapy (TRT criteria)

All of these must be met for sound therapy to support TRT:

- **Never annoying** — especially critical in [[wiki/clinical/hyperacusis]]; the sound must not trigger aversion.
- **Neutral** — should not attract active attention (which would defeat the purpose).
- **Not masking** — tinnitus must remain perceivable within the background; if fully masked, habituation cannot occur.
- **Sufficiently above threshold** — background signal must be strong enough to reduce the contrast; too quiet and the tinnitus-reduction effect is negligible.
- **Extended duration** — at minimum 8 hours per day, continuous; "put on and forget about it."
- **Easy to use and adjust** — patient adherence depends on frictionless delivery.
- **Non-occluding** — occluding ear devices block environmental sounds, reduce speech understanding, increase attentional salience of tinnitus, and can worsen hyperacusis. Open-fit preferred.
- **Bilateral** — unilateral sound therapy may create asymmetric stimulation effects.

## Patient categories and device assignment

TRT patient categorization determines which sound device is appropriate:

| Category | Profile | Device |
|----------|---------|--------|
| **0** | Tinnitus without significant hearing loss; manageable distress | No device needed; environmental sound enrichment only |
| **1** | Tinnitus with distress; no significant hearing loss | Wearable sound generator (white/broadband noise), set below mixing point |
| **2** | Tinnitus + hearing loss | Hearing aid mandatory; may add combination device (HA + sound generator) |
| **3** | Tinnitus + [[wiki/clinical/hyperacusis]] | Focus on hyperacusis first; start at comfortable low level, increase gradually |
| **4** | Tinnitus + severe hyperacusis | Start below hearing threshold; increase very slowly (months); specialist management |

## Delivery and use instructions

- Wear throughout waking hours.
- **Do not adjust initial settings** — consistency is required for the neuroplastic process.
- Instructions: "Put on and forget about it."
- Minimum 8 hours per day.
- Continuous wearing is more effective than intermittent.
- Free-field (environmental) sound enrichment devices during the night (bedside sound machine, fan, etc.).
- Minimum commitment: first 6–12 months of consistent use for habituation to establish.

## Common confusions

- **Sound therapy ≠ music therapy** (in general). Music can be used as "interesting sound" for attention diversion, but active music listening is secondary to the background sound role in TRT.
- **Sound therapy ≠ notched noise therapy.** Notched sound therapy (delivering broadband noise with a notch at the tinnitus frequency to drive lateral inhibition at that frequency) is a distinct experimental approach. Evidence is `low`. Not the same as TRT sound therapy.
- **Category 0 patients do not need a device** — environmental sound enrichment is sufficient. Prescribing a sound generator to all tinnitus patients is incorrect.
- **Occluding earphones are contraindicated** for TRT sound therapy — they block environmental sounds and can worsen the condition.

## App relevance

The app's sound library must support TRT-grade sound therapy. Key requirements:
- Multiple broadband noise types (white, pink, brown, nature sounds) for category 0–1 use.
- Volume control with guidance to stay below the mixing point — the app should educate the user on what the mixing point is and how to find it.
- Duration tracking (8-hour minimum target).
- No features that encourage masking (e.g., "hide your tinnitus" framing is clinically incorrect).
- Night mode / free-field mode for bedside use.
